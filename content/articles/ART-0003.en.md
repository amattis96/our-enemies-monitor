## The question: why is a European mine not enough?

European industry can design and manufacture electric-vehicle motors, wind turbines and industrial equipment. Yet the European Commission estimates that **98% of EU demand for rare-earth magnets is met by Chinese imports** [R1]. The investigative question is therefore more precise than “who owns the mines?”: **at which stage of the value chain is the capacity to constrain supply concentrated, and what is actually changing before 2030?**

Rare earths are not a single commodity. The chain comprises ore mining, concentration, chemical separation of individual elements, refining and metallurgy, alloys, powders, sintered magnet manufacturing and integration into motors and generators. A motor factory in Europe may depend on imported magnets; an Australian mine may ship its concentrate to a third country for separation. These are ordinary economic relationships, not evidence of interference or sabotage [R1][R2].

This investigation compares IEA data for 2024 and 2025, Eurostat figures for 2025, the European Court of Auditors' February 2026 report and industrial decisions announced by the Commission through October 2026 [R2–R7]. **An observed result, an administrative designation and future capacity must not be treated as interchangeable.**

## Six production stages that must not be conflated

| Stage | What it supplies | Exposure to measure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mining | Ore and concentrate | Producing countries, permits and deposit access |
| Separation | Distinct neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium and terbium oxides | Chemical plants, reagents, expertise and yield |
| Metallurgy | Metals, alloys and magnetic powders | Processing capacity and technology availability |
| Magnets | High-performance sintered components | Qualified suppliers, patents, cost and lead times |
| Motors and generators | Integration into the finished product | Manufacturers' bill-of-materials dependence |
| Recovery and substitution | Secondary material and alternative designs | Collection, purity, efficiency and requalification |

The IEA characterises chemical separation as the technically central processing stage; magnet manufacture demands further specialist capabilities. Consequently, sourcing ore from outside China **does not establish** that refined oxide or the finished magnet also comes from outside China [R2][R3].

## Three China percentages, three different denominators

For **2024**, the IEA estimates that China accounted for **60% of world mining of magnet rare earths**, **91% of their refining**, and **94% of global sintered permanent-magnet output** [R2]. These are not China's share of each individual element, nor a measure of EU imports. They describe different stages of a **global value chain**, in the same reference year.

The European Commission instead says that **98% of EU rare-earth magnet demand** is met by Chinese imports, while also recognising European leadership in manufacturing motors and turbines [R1]. This is an indicator of **EU component demand**, not the Chinese share of complete motors sold in Europe.

A third, frequently misunderstood number comes from **Eurostat**. In 2025, the EU imported **15,100 tonnes** of goods classified as rare-earth elements: **46.8% by weight from China**, **25.9% from Russia** and **23.1% from Malaysia**. Exports in this customs category totalled 6,700 tonnes [R4]. The trade basket includes **metals, mixtures and compounds** captured by Combined Nomenclature codes. It is **not a census of finished magnets** or their industrial origin. Thus 46.8%, 94% and 98% are not mathematically contradictory: material, geography, production stage, year and denominator all differ.

## What changed in 2025-2026

The 2024 picture should not automatically be passed off as the situation in November 2026. The **IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026** reports that in 2025, unlike for many other minerals, concentration in rare-earth refining **fell modestly** thanks to new projects in the United States and increased production in Malaysia [R3]. That is meaningful, albeit limited, diversification, not proof that the value chain has become resilient.

In 2025 China imposed export controls on several heavy rare earths, and in October announced further measures affecting connected products and technologies. The IEA reports temporary suspensions of some measures, while also documenting **industrial effects of export restrictions**: some carmakers temporarily reduced activity. These effects do not establish that every shortage was deliberately engineered to harm Europe, or that every restriction remained in force in the same form in November 2026 [R3].

One economically important detail in the IEA's 2026 analysis is the price gap: dysprosium and terbium in Europe were around five times as expensive as on China's domestic market during the period covered [R3]. This is evidence of **market segmentation and an import-security premium**, not a tariff or a causal loss that can automatically be assigned to one specific government measure.

Geopolitical uncertainty can turn ordinary supply concentration into negotiating leverage. **Concentration by itself is not evidence of a particular hostile operation**. Establishing deliberate coercion would require verifiable acts, instructions or conduct, with dates and counter-evidence.

## The auditors' test: targets are not installed capacity

The **Critical Raw Materials Act** sets EU 2030 benchmarks: **10% extraction**, **40% processing**, **25% recycling** of annual consumption of strategic raw materials, and **no more than 65%** supplied by any one third country [R6]. The European Court of Auditors notes that these are **not targets already achieved for every individual material**, nor binding quotas for each substance [R5].

In Special Report 04/2026, the Court finds that import-diversification efforts have yet to produce tangible results; ten of the 26 materials examined are fully imported, while finance, permitting and technology constraints slow progress [R5]. Regarding rare earths, the audit describes processing for EU uses as taking place **outside the Union** in the data it assessed. That is the **audit's finding for its reporting scope and data vintage**, not a claim that no specialist operation or pilot capacity could exist in Europe during 2026. Distinct definitions of separation, refining, recycling and magnet manufacture must be reconciled before comparing facilities.

The Commission reports **106 designated strategic projects overall** under the CRMA, including a second EU list approved on **9 October 2026** with **46 new EU strategic projects** [R7]. Designation brings administrative priority and potential support in obtaining finance; **106 is not the number of operating rare-earth mines, nor is 46 a measure of magnet production already available**. The portfolio covers multiple minerals and different industrial stages.

## Four levers and four tests

**1. Diversify by processing stage, not just the supplier's flag.** Mining investments in Australia, the United States or Europe reduce exposure only if separation plants, metallurgy, magnet production and enforceable contracts are available downstream. Ore sourced outside China but separated and converted inside China leaves the later bottleneck intact [R2][R3].

**2. Verify installed, usable industrial capacity.** Distinguish announced, permitted, under-construction, commissioned and full-rate operations. European manufacturers need tonnes of qualified oxides, metal-processing capacity, certified magnets, competitive energy costs and reliable deliveries. The IEA stresses that the constraint is not only finance: specialist expertise, patented equipment and qualification lead times also matter [R3][R8].

**3. Recycle magnets rather than merely weighing waste.** Recovering rare-earth elements from small components calls for selective collection, dismantling and processes able to retrieve individual elements. The Court criticises recycling indicators based on aggregated waste inputs, which do not show how much neodymium or dysprosium is actually recovered and re-enters supply [R5]. Recycling may contribute progressively but cannot replace primary production overnight.

**4. Substitute where engineering makes it viable.** Some electric motors do not require rare-earth permanent magnets. Choices depend on efficiency, volume, temperature, weight, cost and sector requirements. Substitution can reduce dependence **for a particular design** without making every alternative equivalent, especially in aerospace, defence and high-power-density components [R1][R8].

## Evidence and limits

| Evidence type | What is supported | What does not follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **E1, primary measures and acts** | Eurostat 2025 trade data, CRMA benchmarks and adopted project lists [R4][R6][R7] | That designated projects are producing at full scale |
| **E1/E3, audit and statistics** | ECA Special Report 04/2026 and IEA methodological estimates [R2][R3][R5] | That materials and components share a single denominator |
| **E2, institutional policy** | EU aims to improve autonomy and resilience [R1][R6] | That a company or state committed an offence |
| **E5, strategic hypothesis** | A negotiating vulnerability is possible with heavy concentration | That each licence refusal is a campaign directed at the EU |

These evidence labels classify the **nature of the supporting material**; they do not assign numeric probabilities or a universal ranking. The IEA's 2026 report uses industry data, modelling and market estimates, so some figures depend on coverage assumptions rather than exhaustive customs measurements. Conditional projections are not completed events.

## Questions for the next investigation

1. For each CRMA project involving magnets, which stage does it cover, what is nameplate capacity, and how much saleable output actually left the facility in 2026?
2. What share of the four key magnet elements, Nd, Pr, Dy and Tb, is separated, refined, alloyed and converted into magnets in each country supplying European demand? Customs classifications do not fully trace these transformations [R4][R5].
3. Have export restrictions resulted in specific, documented licence refusals or delays for European customers? A price rise alone is not sufficient evidence of deliberate discrimination.
4. How much recoverable material actually comes from end-of-life magnets rather than aggregated waste categories?
5. Which European motor designs have shifted to rare-earth-free alternatives, and what are their measured performance, cost and materials trade-offs?

These are testable questions, not automatic new graph edges, hypotheses or evidence of coordination.

## Conclusion

The risk is not that “China makes every European motor”. Sources show European strength in several finished products alongside substantial exposure to particular stages of the magnet chain located elsewhere. **Refining and magnet manufacture**, even more than the theoretical availability of deposits, deserve close monitoring. Modest diversification in 2025 and new CRMA project designations are documented progress, **not independence already achieved** [R1–R8].

For Our Enemies Monitor this is a **verifiable strategic dependency**, not automatically an act of infiltration, sabotage or criminal responsibility. The useful metric is not the number of announcements but the real, diversified ability to complete every stage of the supply chain.

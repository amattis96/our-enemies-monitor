## The question: when does a signal become a European security issue?

A civilian aircraft can lose satellite reception without crashing: it has other navigation sources, procedures and air traffic controllers. Yet redundancy does not make interference harmless. The investigative problem has two parts: what evidence establishes operational GNSS degradation in the Baltic, and how precisely can responsibility be attributed? Comparing EASA documents, diplomatic decisions reported by the European Commission and testimony collected by Reuters produces a more disciplined answer than the slogan “Russia switches off GPS”.

GNSS includes constellations such as GPS and Galileo. **Jamming** overwhelms the received signal; **spoofing** makes a receiver process false position or time information. The latter can be more insidious because apparently valid data can propagate into several onboard systems. EASA also describes false terrain awareness and warning system (TAWS) alerts and inappropriate pilot reactions. These are documented technical mechanisms, not proof of who caused each occurrence. [R1][R2]

## Chronology: from managing symptoms to an international response

- **17 March 2022.** EASA publishes the first SIB 2022-02 bulletin on satellite navigation problems; its July 2024 revision will clarify spoofing risks. [R2]
- **25–26 January 2024.** An EASA-IATA workshop brings together more than 120 participants from airlines, manufacturers, authorities and air traffic service providers. Priorities include exchanging incident reports, common procedures and preserving a minimum network of ground-based radio navigation aids. [R1]
- **5 July 2024.** EASA's R3 bulletin warns that false TAWS alerts can trigger uncoordinated climbs and that spoofing is harder to detect than straightforward jamming. [R2]
- **16–17 January 2025.** A Ryanair flight bound for Vilnius diverts to Warsaw following GPS interference on approach. Citing the Lithuanian authority, Reuters reports more than 800 interference reports in the final quarter of 2024, versus 124 in the corresponding quarter of 2023. These are **operational reports**, not 800 individually attributed attacks. [R3]
- **6 June 2025.** Thirteen member states write to the Commission requesting coordinated action on GNSS radio interference. The letter's existence and date are confirmed by the later joint plan, not by an independently verified reconstruction of its signatories here. [R4]
- **3 October 2025.** The Commission reports that the ICAO Assembly condemned Russia over harmful interference. According to the Commission, Estonia, Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland and Sweden presented evidence placing the origin of transmissions on Russian territory. Reuters independently confirms the Assembly decision and reports Russia's denial. [R5][R6]
- **26 March 2026.** EASA and EUROCONTROL publish a European action plan assigning responsibilities and implementation horizons to regulators, controllers, airlines and avionics manufacturers. [R4]
- **3 July 2026.** SIB revision R4 updates pilot-controller phraseology, training and operational measures; the list of affected regions is maintained on a dynamic page rather than permanently fixed in the bulletin. [R7]

This sequence shows a transition from incidents managed in the cockpit and control tower to a systemic risk addressed by European and international institutions. It does not establish that all interference belongs to a single campaign or shares the same motive.

## The chain of evidence: what is known and unknown

**E1, facts and documents.** EASA bulletins, an EASA-EUROCONTROL plan, an ICAO Assembly condemnation reported by the Commission and diversions reported with operational sources exist. One flight diversion does not prove that catastrophe was narrowly avoided; the pilot's alternative response is precisely an intended safety barrier. [R3][R4][R5][R7]

**E2, institutional attribution.** The Commission states that evidence from six countries enabled ICAO to recognise a Russian origin for harmful interference in specified regions. “Originating from Russian territory” is a narrower geospatial and institutional claim than “ordered by a particular Russian service”. The underlying complete technical dossier has not been independently examined here. [R5]

**E3, independent journalism.** Reuters covered the ICAO Assembly, gathered statements from Finnish and Lithuanian officials and reported both the decision and Russian objections. This is an editorial chain separate from the Commission's release, but not an independent radio-frequency measurement of the emitters. [R6]

**E4, allegations and objections.** Estonia and Finland accused Moscow of GPS jamming; Russia rejected those allegations, Reuters reports. ICAO's condemnation is a decision by an international body, not a criminal judgment against identified people or military units. No defendants, convictions or command chains are documented here. [R6]

**E5, open hypotheses.** Electronic warfare associated with defence against drones could plausibly create cross-border side effects. But without geolocated RF measurements, emission characteristics, time logs and operational documentation, it is impossible to determine the intent, target or responsible structure for each occurrence. Unintentional interference is also a technical category envisaged by the European plan. [R4]

## The European mechanism: more than a reception problem

The vulnerability is organisational as well as electromagnetic. Satellite navigation supports more direct routes and approach procedures at airports with less ground infrastructure. When positional integrity becomes uncertain, crews must recognise the anomaly, switch to alternative sources and procedures and coordinate with air traffic control. Safety can be maintained, but airspace capacity and service reliability may decline. EASA and EUROCONTROL say so explicitly. [R4]

The 2026 plan identifies four layers of action: a shared picture of events and data exchange; harmonised instructions for pilots and controllers; coordination between civilian and military authorities; and more resilient avionics over the longer term. The July 2026 R4 revision translates part of that work into operational guidance and training. [R4][R7]

A less visible dependency emerges here than cables or raw materials: Europe's transport system has optimised efficiency and capacity around a satellite service vulnerable to terrestrial disruption. Retaining conventional radio aids is infrastructure insurance, not technological nostalgia. IATA was already calling for it in January 2024. [R1]

## Attribution is a problem, not an automatic exoneration

Locating a radio source does not necessarily identify its operator. An effect observed onboard does not on its own establish an emitter's location. An ICAO decision based on national dossiers carries diplomatic and technical weight, but the underlying evidence must remain distinguishable from its public summary. Conversely, the absence of raw data in this article does not erase incidents or institutional attribution: it limits the degree of independent verification.

At least three interpretations fit some observations: intentional transmissions targeting civilian navigation; military countermeasures directed at other targets with cross-border effects; and a mixture of intentional or accidental sources at different times and places. The consulted material does not permit a quantitative allocation of incidents across these categories. Russia's denial is an objection to record, not positive proof of innocence; a state's accusation is not independent proof of a specific command chain. [R4][R5][R6]

## Missing evidence and the next verification steps

Comparable interference datasets by flight information region, time and type are needed, alongside correlations with terrestrial and satellite RF measurements, maintenance and avionics anomaly logs, common definitions distinguishing an event, a report and an affected flight, the technical dossiers submitted to ICAO, and documented diversion and delay costs. Without these, an increase in reports must not be converted into an estimate of economic damage or a count of attributed attacks.

The next question is not only “who switched on the jammer?” but “how much civilian redundancy remains when part of the sky no longer supplies reliable coordinates?”. Europe's 2026 response acknowledges the issue and allocates action, but publishing an action plan does not itself establish that every measure has been implemented. [R4][R7]

## Conclusion

GNSS operational risk in European aviation is documented; the technical and regulatory response can be verified; the international condemnation of Russia is a diplomatic fact. Institutional localisation of interference and the responsibility of individual units or people are different propositions. Treating them as equivalent would weaken the investigation the case demands. Air traffic resilience will depend as much on the ability to attribute sources as on the ability to fly safely when attribution is impossible. [R1][R4][R5][R7]

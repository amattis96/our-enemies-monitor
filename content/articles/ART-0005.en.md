## The question: what actually makes the shadow fleet vulnerable?

A tanker leaving a Russian port is not, for that reason alone, an illegal ship. Nor is a sanctioned vessel automatically responsible for sabotage. The crucial question is different: which checks remain feasible when ownership, flag, insurance, commercial operator and cargo fall under different jurisdictions? The so-called “shadow fleet” is a heterogeneous group, not an organisation with a proven single command. Definitions differ among authorities and researchers. The European Union targets specific ships and actors, not every vessel trading Russian oil [R1, R2].

This investigation cross-examines four distinct dimensions: economic restrictions, maritime document fraud, powers of intervention at sea and infrastructure security. The distinction matters because the same ships may appear in discussions of sanctions, the environment and sabotage without evidence of a causal connection among those phenomena.

## A chronology of rules, not just incidents

**June 2024.** The EU's fourteenth sanctions package introduces port-access and maritime-service restrictions for specific vessels. Designation depends on EU legal acts, not a journalistic label [R1].

**14 November 2024.** The European Parliament calls for stronger action against ships, owners, managers, insurers and intermediaries. This is Parliament's political position, not proof that every actor mentioned broke the law [R3].

**20 May 2025.** Under the seventeenth package, the Council adds 189 vessels, bringing the number then designated to 342. It also sanctions operators and an insurer linked to the sector. The same announcement attributes an effect on Russian revenue to the measures; its aggregate figure does not isolate the impact of each designation [R2].

**15 December 2025.** The Council adopts a declaration on using the law of the sea to address shadow-fleet threats and protect undersea infrastructure. On the same day it sanctions five people and four entities identified as enablers. The political declaration does not itself create a general power to board any ship [R4, R5].

**17 April 2026.** The International Maritime Organization (IMO) Legal Committee approves voluntary guidelines for more transparent registration. The IMO reports 529 ships falsely flying a flag over the preceding year and fraudulent use of flags involving nearly 40 states. This is a global figure, not a count of the Russian shadow fleet [R6].

**23 July 2026.** The EU's twenty-first package adds 41 vessels to the 632 already listed and broadens its attention to support services, including bunkering. The resulting total, 673, refers to the EU list at that date, not the worldwide size of the shadow fleet [R7].

**2026.** Belgium's intervention involving the tanker Ethera, reported in the press as involving suspected false-flag operation and irregular documents, illustrates that operational action needs a concrete legal basis. Allegations remain allegations until determined by a competent authority [R8].

## The mechanism: five identities for one ship

The **physical vessel** has technical characteristics and an IMO identifier; its **flag** determines its legal nationality; the **beneficial owner** may differ from the registered owner; the **manager** organises operations; the **insurer** assumes risks and liabilities. Charterers and cargo origin add further layers. A check that looks only at a commercial name may fail to reconstruct the full chain.

The IMO describes at least three fraudulent practices: continuing to use a flag after registration ends, submitting false documents to maritime bodies and transmitting manipulated AIS data. These are not interchangeable: an AIS interruption may have technical causes, whereas a fraudulent registration calls for confirmation from the flag-state authority [R6, R9]. A legitimate flag change is not a false flag. Using special-purpose companies does not by itself establish sanctions evasion.

The new IMO guidelines propose checks on ownership, identity and registry authorisation. They are voluntary: their approval is neither a binding treaty nor evidence that every state has implemented them [R6].

## Three systems that must not be conflated

**Economic sanctions (E1).** EU decisions prohibit specified operations and services involving designated vessels and actors. Effectiveness depends on identification, enforcement and cooperation with operators and third countries. A ban does not automatically make a ship subject to confiscation everywhere [R1, R2, R7].

**Maritime safety (E1/E2).** Fraudulent registries, opaque insurance and substandard ships can increase accident risks and complicate liability for damage. Parliament and Council express these concerns; quantifying the additional risk for an individual ship would require inspection, classification, maintenance and insurance data that are not always public [R3, R4, R6].

**Strategic security (E2/E5).** European institutions warn of potential threats to undersea infrastructure. Yet damage to a cable, a vessel's presence nearby and possible shadow-fleet membership are three separate facts. None alone identifies a perpetrator, sponsor or intention to sabotage [R4]. Collapsing these layers would produce a stronger accusation than the available evidence supports.

## The legal bottleneck: who can board?

An EU designation can restrict port access and services within the applicable legal order. On the high seas, however, flag-state jurisdiction, recognised international-law exceptions and the consent of the competent state become relevant. The EU declaration of December 2025 invokes existing tools and cooperation, not an unrestricted licence to seize tankers [R4].

Reuters reported in October 2025 on a diplomatic proposal for agreements with flag states to facilitate pre-authorised inspections: the need for such agreements itself indicates that the power cannot simply be assumed [R10]. Inspection, detention, cargo seizure and final forfeiture also need to be distinguished: each has different legal prerequisites. Belgium's 2026 operation requires examination in its judicial context, not treatment as a precedent making all future interceptions automatically lawful [R8].

## Money, oil and the limits of measurement

The European rationale is to reduce Russia's ability to earn energy revenue that can also finance its war. The Commission and Council report progress and progressively expand their lists [R1, R2, R7]. But three different numbers must not be added together: designated ships, ships considered “shadow” by analysts and voyages actually made. A vessel can change its name or flag without becoming a new ship; designation may not prevent every voyage to third-country markets.

A serious causal evaluation would compare volumes, discounts, freight costs, insurance, routes and tax revenue before and after each measure, controlling for global oil prices and Asian demand. The Council's figures are institutional indicators, not an independent estimate of the net effect of fleet-specific measures alone [R2]. Trade law can also allow a product refined in a third country to acquire an origin different from that of the crude: a Commission answer in June 2025 expressly distinguished this from a violation of sanctions then in force [R11]. Later rules must be checked against the date of each transaction.

## Challenges and competing interpretations

The European position treats the network as a threat to sanctions, the environment and security. Moscow disputes the approach taken by Western interdictions; the freedom-of-navigation argument deserves legal scrutiny rather than dismissal by political label [R10, R12]. An alternative interpretation is that some ownership, flag or insurance changes respond to ordinary commercial needs and the withdrawal of Western operators: without specific evidence, not every transfer can be classified as fraud.

The sources have limits too: Council and Parliament are primary for their own decisions, not independent assessments of their success; IMO is primary on registrations, but its 529 cases are not collectively attributed to Russia; Reuters and other reporting describe interventions and positions, not substitutes for court files or certified ship registries.

## What European monitoring should measure

Each vessel needs a verifiable history of IMO number, flags with dates and registry confirmation, manager and beneficial owner, classification society, P&I insurance, ports, ship-to-ship transfers, inspections and sanctions acts with effective dates. Every anomaly should identify its original source and whether that source is independent of others.

The priority is not to build a graph in which every suspicious tanker automatically points to an intelligence service. It is to make the difference between **administrative designation**, **verified documentary irregularity**, **incident**, **sabotage allegation** and **established responsibility** searchable. That distinction enables European comparisons while reducing defamation risks and false positives.

## Open questions and conclusion

How many designated ships have actually lost access to services and ports? Which flag states have confirmed fraudulent registrations, and through which acts? Have the new IMO guidelines produced measurable operational checks? Which incidents have competent authorities linked to technical deficiencies, and which only to sabotage suspicions? How many inspections have led to final orders?

The shadow fleet exposes a problem of **cross-border verifiability**: sanctioning a ship is easier than continuously establishing its identity, control, coverage and conduct. Institutions have expanded their tools, but public sources do not support inferring a single criminal command structure or involvement in sabotage by every opaque vessel. The strongest investigative finding is the documented gap between economic lists, ship registries and legal powers of intervention.

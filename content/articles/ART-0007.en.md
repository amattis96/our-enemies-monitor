## The question: what does an anchor on the seabed actually prove?

Three ships, damaged cables, criminal investigations, different decisions. Between December 2024 and November 2026 the Baltic became an unintended testing ground for European infrastructure security. The question is not merely who broke a connection, but **what chain of evidence can distinguish a maritime accident, knowing misconduct and an operation directed by a state**.

Comparing Eagle S, Vezhen and Fitburg reveals a progression often lost in headlines: physical contact between an anchor and a cable can be established without proving intent; intent attributed to crew members does not necessarily identify a sponsor; even the power to try a case depends on the law of the sea. The same events can therefore warrant immediate protective measures without supporting an allegation of state sabotage.

This is a comparative investigation of public documents and differing evidentiary thresholds, not geopolitical attribution by association.

## Comparative chronology: from suspicion to decisions

**25 December 2024, Eagle S.** The Estlink 2 electricity interconnector and four telecommunications cables in the Gulf of Finland are damaged. Finnish police reconstruct a drag mark on the seabed and, in January 2025, assess that the recovered anchor belongs to the Eagle S tanker. This physical finding matters but does not by itself establish an intentional order [1, 2].

**14 January 2025, Baltic Sentry.** NATO announces increased surveillance involving frigates, aircraft and uncrewed technologies. Its Secretary General explicitly separates the Alliance's surveillance and deterrence role from national police and judicial powers. This is a security response to risk, not a verdict on every incident [3].

**26 January 2025, Vezhen.** A cable between Latvia and Sweden's Gotland island is damaged. The Malta-flagged vessel is investigated. On 3 February the Swedish prosecutor rules out intentional sabotage; on 13 October 2025 the investigation is definitively closed. The reconstruction identifies adverse weather, faulty anchor safety mechanisms and deficiencies in seamanship. Evidence of a dragging anchor is therefore consistent with an accident, not just an attack [4, 5].

**21 February 2025, European response.** The Commission and High Representative present a communication covering five functions: prevent, detect, respond, repair and deter. Network protection is not made conditional on a judicial resolution of every incident [6].

**11 August and 3 October 2025, Eagle S.** Finnish prosecutors charge the captain and two officers with aggravated criminal damage and serious interference with communications, alleging that an anchor was dragged for roughly 90 km. The three deny the offences and dispute Finnish jurisdiction because the damage occurred outside territorial waters. In October the District Court dismisses the case for lack of jurisdiction: a procedural ruling, not an acquittal on the merits [1, 7].

**27 August 2026, Eagle S reversal.** The Helsinki Court of Appeal overturns the jurisdiction ruling and sends the case back to the District Court. According to two specialist legal analyses, the decisive issue is how the conduct is classified under Article 97 of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea: an initial loss of an anchor may be accidental, but knowingly continuing after warnings from authorities may no longer constitute a mere incident of navigation. The court did not find the defendants guilty [8, 9].

**31 December 2025 and 5 November 2026, Fitburg.** Two telecommunications cables between Finland and Estonia are damaged. The Helsinki District Court convicts the captain and boatswain at first instance of aggravated sabotage, finding they knew the anchor was dragging for approximately 130 km. Sentences are three years and six months and two years and ten months respectively. Both deny wrongdoing; the captain announces an appeal. The ruling is not final and does not prove an order from any government [10, 11].

## Three files, three different evidentiary steps

| Case | Physical contact and mechanism | Assessment of intent | Judicial position on 23 November 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eagle S | Seabed drag mark and anchor attributed by investigators to the vessel | Criminal conduct alleged and disputed by the defence | Jurisdiction recognised on appeal; merits unresolved in consulted sources |
| Vezhen | Ship's anchor found responsible for damage | Swedish prosecution: accidental combination of weather, defects and poor seamanship | Investigation closed without sabotage charges |
| Fitburg | Anchor chain and shank dragged for about 130 km | Finnish court: awareness of the conduct | First-instance conviction, subject to appeal |

These differences do not merely reflect political judgements. Telemetry, anchor positions, mechanical locking devices, radio communications, warnings received, crew reactions and the duration of dragging all matter. **Continuing after a warning** can change the legal interpretation of an event that began by mistake. Yet each file must be judged on its own evidence: Fitburg's verdict does not fill gaps in Eagle S, and it does not reopen Vezhen.

## Four levels that must not be conflated

**E1, fact or document:** a cable is interrupted, an anchor recovered, a prosecution filed, a judicial ruling issued. The damage and existence of an official document can be established, but not necessarily every assertion within it.

**E2, institutional attribution:** investigators and courts attribute particular conduct to ships or individuals on the basis of technical data. Attribution must specify the author, date, scope and appeal status.

**E3, independent reconstruction:** Reuters, Yle and maritime-law specialists provide outside scrutiny, but multiple accounts of the same judgement do not become independent proof of the underlying conduct.

**E4, allegation or challenge:** prosecutors allege intent; defendants deny it; owners and defence counsel raise technical or jurisdictional objections. The allegation exists as a fact, but its merits cannot be presumed.

**E5, hypothesis:** an intelligence operation or state coordination would require orders, communications, financing or other verifiable evidence. Flag, crew nationality, departure port, Russian cargo or participation in the so-called shadow fleet **are not sufficient evidence** of direction from Moscow. None of the decisions examined publicly identifies a state chain of command.

## The legal hinge: exclusive economic zones and Article 97

Eagle S illustrates a concrete limitation on European protection. Cables cross exclusive economic zones, where coastal states do not exercise the same sovereignty as in territorial waters. The defence invokes international rules governing jurisdiction over incidents of navigation; prosecutors point to effects in Finland. As described by legal specialists, the 2026 appeal ruling distinguishes an initially fortuitous event from knowingly continuing the conduct. It addresses whether a trial can proceed, not a final finding of criminal liability [8, 9].

This yields an operational distinction: intercepting a vessel to prevent further damage, collecting forensic evidence and prosecuting individual officers are three actions governed by different powers and thresholds. NATO surveillance does not replace national investigative authority, nor does a sanctions designation substitute for proof of an offence.

## What changes for Europe

Subsea infrastructure is interdependent: a single break may be absorbed by redundant links, while simultaneous failures may compound costs, restoration delays and systemic risk. Finnish prosecutors estimated at least EUR 60 million in immediate repair costs in Eagle S; that is a case-specific prosecutorial estimate, not the total cost of all Baltic incidents [1].

The EU strategy and Baltic Sentry address risk before criminal responsibility is settled: more monitoring, information sharing, repair capacity, network resilience and deterrence. Assessing effectiveness requires comparable public indicators: mean detection times, proportion of failures technically explained, repair times, alternative route availability and final judicial outcomes. Without such data, each new failure cannot automatically prove deterrence has failed, nor can fewer incidents alone establish that an operation has succeeded.

## Missing evidence and competing explanations

A coordinated sabotage interpretation would require a verifiable link between individual conduct and an external decision-making centre. The sources examined do not supply that link. An independent-accidents interpretation fits Vezhen but cannot automatically be extended to Fitburg, where a court found intent. Gross negligence is another possible explanation, distinct from both unavoidable error and deliberate action.

Outstanding questions include the final outcome of appeals, access to complete court files and shipboard data, comparability of forensic reconstructions, and whether evidence of external sponsors becomes available. The public absence of such evidence does not prove it does not exist; it does prevent its presentation as established.

## Conclusion: protect without prejudging

The three cases show that **damage, intent, jurisdiction and sponsorship are separate questions**. Vezhen requires acknowledging an accident when evidence points to one. Eagle S shows that jurisdiction can be disputed even when physical damage is established. Fitburg shows that a court may find intentional conduct without finding state direction, and that a first-instance verdict remains appealable.

The strongest European response combines preventive protection and evidentiary discipline: neither dismiss vulnerabilities nor turn every anchor into proof of hybrid warfare.
